I want to learn about the following topic:

How context windows work: why long AI conversations can go wrong, and what to do about it.

Please write a document to teach me about this.

Who it is for: me. I am an executive. I use Claude most days for drafting, summarizing and thinking through decisions. I am not technical and I do not want to become technical.

Why I want it: some of my long conversations drift. The model forgets what we agreed earlier, repeats itself, or gets vaguer as the thread grows. I want to understand why that happens and what to do differently, so I can explain it to my team in my own words.

What I already know: the model reads my whole conversation each time it replies. I have heard the terms "token" and "context window" but I could not define either one precisely.

What the document should cover:

- why quality can drop before the limit is reached

The form: a self-contained document under 900 words, with short headings and a glossary of no more than six terms at the end. Plain language, short sentences. Where you give a number, give the source. If you are not sure of a number, say so rather than guess.

How Context Windows Work

A short guide for non-technical users who live in long threads.

What a token is

A token is a chunk of text — usually a word, a piece of a word, or a punctuation mark. As a rough rule, 100 tokens is about 75 English words.

Everything you type and everything the model writes back is counted in tokens.

What a context window is

The context window is the total amount of text the model can hold in view at one time. It covers the whole conversation: your first message, every reply since, any documents you pasted or uploaded, and the instructions sitting behind the scenes.

The model has no memory outside that window. Each time it replies, it re-reads the whole thread from the start. There is no separate place where "what we agreed earlier" is stored unless it is still inside the window.

Size: Anthropic's documentation lists a 200,000-token context window for its current Claude models, with a 1,000,000-token option available on some models. 200,000 tokens is roughly 150,000 words — a long book.

A caution on the number: model names and limits change every few months, and features are sometimes in beta or restricted to certain plans. Treat 200,000 as the working figure and check Anthropic's official docs (docs.anthropic.com, "Models overview") before you quote it to your team. I would not state a precise figure for a specific product tier without checking it that day.

What happens when you exceed the window

Two things can happen, depending on the product.

In a chat interface, the oldest part of the conversation gets dropped or compressed to make room. Your early messages quietly fall off the front. The model is not being careless — that text is simply no longer there. This is the usual cause of "we agreed on this three hours ago and now it's gone."

In other setups, you get an error instead and the request fails.

Either way, the practical point is the same: old content in a very long thread is not reliable.

Why quality drops before you hit the limit

This is the part most people miss. You rarely run out of room. You run out of clarity first.

Dilution. A 40-message thread contains your good instructions plus every abandoned draft, tangent and correction. The signal-to-noise ratio falls. The model is weighing all of it.

Contradictions. You said "keep it formal" in message 3 and "loosen it up" in message 20. Both are in view. The model has to guess which wins, and it often splits the difference — which is how you get vagueness.

Middle-of-the-thread neglect. Research on long-context models has repeatedly found that information placed in the middle of a long input is used less reliably than information at the beginning or end. The paper usually cited is "Lost in the Middle: How Language Models Use Long Contexts" (Liu et al., 2023). Directionally this is well established; how strongly it applies to any specific current model, I can't tell you precisely.

Self-imitation. The model's own earlier replies are part of what it reads. If it hedged once, it sees a hedging example and hedges again. Long threads get more repetitive and more generic by a kind of echo effect.

Habits to start this week

1. Treat a thread as one task. One thread, one decision or one document. When the task changes, open a new thread. Long threads are not a sign of good work; they're usually a sign that three jobs got tangled together.

2. Restate the brief at the top of every new thread. Objective, audience, constraints, tone, what "done" looks like. Ten lines. This is the single highest-return habit.

3. Put standing knowledge somewhere reusable. Anything true across many conversations — your team's structure, your writing style rules, board reporting conventions, recurring acronyms — should not be re-typed each time. Use Claude's Projects feature (project instructions and attached files), a saved brief document you paste in, or a personal-preferences setting if your plan offers one. Then each thread starts informed instead of starting cold.

4. Summarize and jump. When a thread gets unwieldy, ask: "Summarize the decisions we've made, the constraints, the open questions, and the current best draft. Write it so I can paste it into a fresh conversation." Check the summary. Fix it. Open a new thread and paste it in. You have kept the conclusions and discarded the noise.

Do this at natural breaks, not at crisis point. A good trigger: when you notice the first repetition.

5. Put what matters at the end. Before an important request, re-paste the current draft and the key constraints. Don't rely on the model reaching back twenty messages. Recency helps.

6. Correct by replacing, not layering. Instead of "actually make it shorter," say "ignore the previous instructions on length; the target is 400 words." This reduces the contradiction problem.

7. Watch for the three warning signs. Repetition, vagueness, and drift from an agreed decision. Any one of them means the thread is spent. Summarize and start fresh — don't push through.

The one-line version for your team

The model re-reads the whole conversation every time, it has no memory beyond that, and long messy threads make its job harder — so work in short focused threads, restate the brief, keep standing facts in a Project, and summarize-and-restart at the first sign of drift.

Glossary

  • Token — a chunk of text, roughly ¾ of a word.
  • Context window — all the text the model can see at once; the whole thread plus attachments.
  • Truncation — dropping the oldest text when the window fills.
  • Lost in the middle — the tendency to use information buried mid-thread less reliably.
  • Project — a Claude workspace holding instructions and files that apply to every thread inside it.
  • Handoff summary — a condensed brief you carry from a spent thread into a fresh one.
